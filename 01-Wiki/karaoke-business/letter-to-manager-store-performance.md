# Letter to the Manager — Store Performance

**Summary:** A long, angry letter written to the manager arguing that the store's decline comes from decisions made on assumptions instead of numbers. It works through comps, advertising spend, food cost, the website, and how the venue compares to Manhattan and Brooklyn competitors. Written mostly in Korean with a calmer English section at the end that summarizes the whole case. This is the most detailed business analysis in the notes.

## Key Points
- Core charge: **decisions are made emotionally or from old habits instead of from numbers.**
- **$1,700 was spent on ads** without the results being checked.
- Instagram ads at ~$40–50/week (~$160/month) brought **zero customers and zero bookings** — but one organic post with no ad spend brought in a package sale, and another brought a team that spent ~$1,000.
- **1,000–2,000 people clicked through to the website and did not book** — the site had not been changed in 7–8 years.
- A ten-year manager not knowing **liquor cost** or **food cost** is called out as the central problem.
- Sales dropped by roughly **half** over the three years the manager had near-total control.

## The Argument About Comps
The letter pushes back hard on being told not to give service drinks.

Personally, comping a stranger gains him nothing — no tip follows it, since tips are already collected either way. What it does create is more work: more cleaning, more dishes, more drinks to make, more restocking. The only upside is a small increase in the chance of raising sales.

When he *does* give a service drink, he qualifies the customer first — all of it done while walking them to their room:
- How did they get here, and how did they hear about us?
- When were they last here? If it's been more than 9 months, why?
- Do they live nearby? What do they do? Do they have money to spend here?
- Do they have a big friend group — are they worth referrals?

Only if giving them something makes it meaningful to invite them back does the comp make sense.

The counter-example given: on the day he was criticized for giving two cocktails to friends, that group of three was **the only group that spent money in the store all day** — about $200 total. His friends average $200–$400 per group, and they come because they know he works there, not because he invited them.

The conclusion drawn: with rooms sitting empty, taking anything is better than taking nothing. *"If the room is empty, whatever comes in, whatever we give away for free, earning something is better."* Refusing that is refusing to make money.

## The Argument About Advertising
- **$1,700 in ad spend** with the results never checked. For someone who has managed for ten years, not knowing the outcome of one of the biggest expenses is described as inconceivable.
- In the month they started counting, total sales were roughly **$3,000–$4,000**, and Google accounted for only **20–30%** of that.
- Instagram ads: about **$40–$50 per week**, roughly **$160/month**. Customers who came from those ads: **none**. Bookings from those ads: **none**.
- Meanwhile, **one Instagram post with no ad spend** brought in a team that spent around **$1,000**.
- **1,000–2,000 people clicked through to the website.** They were interested enough to click — then changed their mind at the site itself, because of the design, the state of it, or the prices.

The point: when sales are low, that is exactly when marketing has to be used properly, not cut.

## The Argument About Food Cost
Told to stop making food because it costs money, he worked out the actual cost:
- A box of chicken makes a box of fried chicken. The ingredients added — flour, pepper, seasoning — come to **less than a dollar**.
- Labor should not be counted, because the chicken gets made during downtime when there is nothing else to do. Standing there or frying chicken costs the same.
- Labor only counts if you would have to hire an extra person to do the work.

## The Market Comparison
The store keeps being run as though it competes with Manhattan and Brooklyn venues. The letter argues it competes with **Flushing**.

- Lower East Side / Orchard Street, Williamsburg and Bushwick venues sit on busy streets. Rent there might be **$5,000** against roughly **$2,500** here for the equivalent space.
- Customers in those neighbourhoods have close to **double** the disposable income for a night out.
- Those areas are walk-up, with short Uber hops. Here, there is parking and almost nobody walks — and once someone is in a car, coming here or driving to Flushing is about the same 10–15 minutes.

## The Blunt Assessment
> Of all the karaoke places in New York City, Christmas is the worst, in my view. The rooms aren't good, the sound isn't good, the price isn't good, the mics aren't good. And we don't even give service.
>
> So other than BYOB — what reason do customers have to come here? Name two.

## Other Incidents Cited
- **The reservation cancellation:** blamed for replying an hour or two late, but the customer actually cancelled a **month later**. The dates were never checked.
- **The lost post-it note:** a five-to-ten minute complaint about losing a post-it with the Korean karaoke room update info. His response — if it was that important, why was it on a post-it and not written where important things get written? The adhesive stops sticking after a while.
- **The review rule:** the manager's own rule was that leaving a review earns a free shot.
- Being told *"you're not allowed to give service"* by text after comping two cocktails.

## The English Closing Statement
The letter ends in English, and this section is the cleanest version of the whole argument:

> The pattern I noticed is that whenever you blame me for a problem, I look into the numbers to see what I would need to fix. But when I check the actual cause, the problem usually comes from decisions being made without doing the math first.
>
> For example, when you said expenses were high because I was giving service, I calculated what I would actually have to give away for that to make a serious difference. The amount would be unrealistic: dozens of beers, many bottles of soju, or a huge number of shots or mixed drinks. That means the expense problem is not being explained correctly.
>
> The same thing happened when you said BYOB was more profitable than packages. I believed there was a chance you were right because you have been in this business for many years. But when I looked at it, the numbers did not support that.
>
> Food is another example. When you said we should stop making food because it was costing money, that was also said without a full calculation of cost, sales, customer value, and whether food helps customers stay longer or spend more.
>
> The same problem happens with advertising. Google Ads should be bringing in new customers. Instagram and package ads should be tested based on actual sales, reservations, and customer response. One Instagram post without ads already brought in package sales. Instead of testing it properly, the idea was dismissed too quickly because expenses were high. But when sales are low, that is exactly when marketing needs to be used correctly.
>
> I also do not think it is fair to say customers do not come when I work. Last week, my sales were higher than Sula's sales. When I averaged the weekday numbers, my average was higher. So the "bad luck" explanation does not match the sales data.
>
> The bigger problem is that decisions are being made based on assumptions instead of numbers. If we want the store to improve, we need to stop guessing and start tracking sales, expenses, ads, packages, BYOB, food, and staff performance properly.
>
> I am not saying this to argue. I am saying this because the store keeps losing opportunities when decisions are made emotionally or based on old habits instead of actual numbers.

## Note on Tone
The Korean sections are considerably angrier than the English closing, with a lot of profanity. If any of this is ever going to be sent or used, the English section stands on its own and makes every substantive point without the heat.
