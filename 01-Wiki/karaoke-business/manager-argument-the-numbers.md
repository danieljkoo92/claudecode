# Manager Argument — The Numbers

**Summary:** The second and more numerical half of the argument with the manager. It works out exactly how many free drinks it would take to move expenses at all, lays out the math behind comping as marketing, lists everything the manager does not calculate, and compares weekday sales performance directly. It also covers the package redesign reasoning and two past incidents about money.

## Key Points
- To raise expenses by **$30**, he would have to give away **250 free shots**.
- To raise expenses by **$100**: **14 bottles** — one box — or **500 shots**, or **1,500 shooters**.
- Comp math: a cocktail costs about **$1**. Give 30 away, **1 person returns and you break even. 2 and you double.**
- Package sales average roughly **5 people a week**.
- Last month the boss cleared **$650**; $700–800 of the expenses should have been profit.
- On weekday averages he outsold Sua — despite working the **three slowest days** to her two.

## The Comp Math
The core argument, laid out plainly:

> If a cocktail costs $1, and you give it to 30 people and one of them comes back, you're even. If two come back, you're doubling your money.

And the same for shots:

> It's 250 shots to make your money back. You don't think 5–10 people out of 250 will come back — and come back more frequently — if I drink a shot with them and get cool with them?

The scaled version:

> If we give drinks to 60 people and one group of seven brings one extra person, that one person pays for all 60 shots. Because those 60 shots left everyone a little bit happier — say 1% happier.

He also notes the personal cost runs the other way: giving free drinks means more dishes, more drinks to make, more cleaning, and no tip benefit since gratuity is already charged. *"I don't benefit from giving people a free shot. If anything, I lose."*

## What Doesn't Get Calculated
A direct list from the letter — things the manager does not track:

- How much is made off drinks
- How much a package makes
- Why sales are high or low
- Why the boss's profits were low
- Where customers are coming from
- What advertising costs per person, and whether it made or wasted money
- An average ROI — the percentage of sales the owner should normally clear
- Even the "born in an unlucky year" explanation would be believable with actual data behind it

## The Weekday Comparison
He averaged his last three weeks of weekday sales against Sua's and sold more.

The schedules make the gap larger than it looks:
- **Sua:** Wednesday and Thursday — one busy day, one slow day.
- **Him:** Sunday, Monday and Tuesday — one busy day and **two of the slowest days there are**.

If the "customers don't come when he works" theory were true, he should be roughly a third *lower*, not higher.

## Why the Packages Were Redesigned
Not only to look good for customers. The bigger reason:

> It's also to show the other employees that you give a shit about selling it. When you make everything cheap and do everything cheap, it looks like you don't even care if it sells.

## On BYOB vs Packages Again
The manager's argument was that BYOB customers stay in the room longer. The counter: **nobody stays five hours**, and no small group stays four. Meanwhile drink sales run close to **100% profit**. Package 2 is $25 with about $5 of drinks in it.

## Two Money Incidents
Raised directly, including the part that reflects badly on him:

1. **The $70** — taken without thinking early on. Called his own mistake, plainly.
2. **The friend's card** — when a bill got mixed up, he paid with his friend's card and wrote it on the bill paper and the receipts. None of the boss's money was missing; one second of checking would have shown that.

He also notes spending $160–200 of **his own money** on Korean ads.

## Correlation vs Causation
On being told repeatedly "when John was here, sales were this":

> It shouldn't be *when* John was here, sales were that. It should be *because* John was here, sales were that. Why correlate things that don't matter? The only reason they'd matter is if you're looking at random luck, because you don't know how to calculate anything else.

## The Structural Point
Two observations that stand on their own:

- **The facility gets older and more outdated every year.** Even doing everything else identically, sales decline. That is not a staffing problem.
- **Of all the locations, the one in the bad spot is the only one doing badly.** That suggests the setup only works where foot traffic carries it — and that nobody has learned to raise sales without it.

## Also Noted
- Told the FDNY inspection was "not important, don't worry about it."
- The manager's reaction to rooms not being cleaned before an inspection was that he felt betrayed — the response being that the real betrayal was nobody being on top of the store at all.
