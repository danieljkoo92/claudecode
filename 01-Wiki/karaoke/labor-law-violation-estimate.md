# Labor Law Violation Estimate

**Summary:** A rough calculation of what the employer's labor law violations would be worth if pursued — unpaid overtime, spread-of-hours pay, missing wage notices and missing paystubs. The estimate lands at $15,000–30,000 and the note observes that the number keeps rising the more it is calculated, because nearly everything is being violated. It also flags that withholding a paycheck until a signature is obtained is itself a violation.

## Key Points
- Estimate: **$15,000–$30,000**, and rising the longer it is worked out.
- **Unpaid overtime:** ~250–300 hours × $8.50 = $2,550, **doubled to $5,100** (liquidated damages).
- **Spread of hours:** ~50 days × $17 = $850, **doubled to $1,700**.
- **No wage notice at hiring:** up to **$5,000**.
- **No paystubs:** $250 per day, up to **$5,000**.
- **Withholding a paycheck pending a signature is itself illegal**, regardless of the reason.

## The Calculation
| Violation | Basis | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid overtime | 300 hrs × $8.50 = $2,550, ×2 | **$5,100** |
| Spread of hours (extra hour for each day over 10) | ~50 days × $17 = $850, ×2 | **$1,700** |
| No wage notice on hiring | Statutory | **up to $5,000** |
| No paystubs provided | $250/day | **up to $5,000** |
| | **Rough total** | **$15,000–30,000** |

## On the Withheld Paycheck
> Do you know that not giving me my weekly pay because you need a signature this weekend is also a labor law violation? Not paying wages on the payday for any reason is a violation. Saying you won't pay until you get a signature is absolutely a violation.
>
> Does it make sense for the person managing the store not to know labor law?

The related point made elsewhere: the paperwork being demanded is worth little anyway, since nothing was signed for around two years — *"whatever you're collecting right now is useless."*

## The Prediction
> If something blows up over this, they'll probably say again — "this never happened in ten years" — and talk like it isn't their fault.

## Important Caveats
This is a self-made estimate written in anger, not legal advice, and three things about it need checking before it is relied on:

- **The overtime rate.** $8.50 appears to be a half-time differential rather than the full overtime premium. Depending on the actual base rate, the real figure could be substantially higher.
- **Liquidated damages are not automatic.** New York allows doubling, but an employer who shows good faith can avoid it. Doubling everything up front produces an optimistic number.
- **There are deadlines.** New York's statute of limitations for wage claims is six years, but federal claims are shorter. Waiting has a cost.

**Talk to an employment attorney before quoting any of these figures to the employer.** Most take wage cases on contingency, so an initial consultation costs nothing — and a number backed by a lawyer is worth far more in a negotiation than one worked out alone.
