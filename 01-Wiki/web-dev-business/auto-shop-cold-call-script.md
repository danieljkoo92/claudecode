# Auto Shop Cold Call Script

**Summary:** A full cold call script for selling websites to auto repair shops, built on a true story — helping a girlfriend fix her brakes, finding a competitor's website with price ranges, and going there instead. The pitch opens with "you lost me as a customer for around $500" and closes by offering a free website concept that has already been built. The strongest argument in it is that a single repair job can pay for the whole site.

## Key Points
- Opens with a **real story**, not a sales line: "you lost me as a customer for around $500."
- The competitor won because their website listed **basic price ranges for common repairs**.
- Key argument: a restaurant site needs many customers to pay for itself; an auto shop site needs **one**.
- Prices are low because he freelances — no salesperson, project manager or designer splitting the fee.
- Closes by offering a **free, already-built website concept** with no obligation.
- Fallback ask: even a "no" should give feedback on the design, which improves the next client's version.

## The Script

**Opening:**
> "Hi, my name is Daniel. I know this might seem random, but I came across your shop a couple of weeks ago when I had to help my girlfriend fix her brakes. Basically, I'll put it straightforward: you lost me as a customer for around $500. Would you like to hear the reason why?"

**If they say yes:**
> "So I found another auto shop with a website that had basic price ranges for common repairs. Their range for the brake work was lower than the two quotes I had already gotten over the phone, so I called them just to confirm it applied to her car.
>
> Honestly, having that information on the website made it feel easier to trust them in that moment. I did not have to call another shop completely blind and start the whole conversation over again.
>
> And now that I've been looking at auto shops more, I've noticed a lot of them do not have websites at all. Usually, when I'm looking into any business, the first place I go is their website. When I could not find one for you guys, it threw me off a little and made me choose the other place instead."

**The transition:**
> "So the reason I'm calling is that I build websites for local businesses as a side job. Before you think I'm just calling to sell you something — I usually work with bars, restaurants, places like that. I've never actually designed one for an auto shop before. But after dealing with my girlfriend's situation, it made me realize something."

**The core argument:**
> "For a restaurant, a website has to bring in a lot of extra customers before it really pays for itself. But for an auto shop, it could take just one customer. If one person finds your shop, trusts the website enough to call you, and brings in one decent repair job, that could cover the entire cost of the website. Every customer after that is basically extra money the website brought in for you.
>
> And I charge extremely reasonable prices compared to regular web-design companies because I freelance independently. I'm not splitting the price between a salesperson, project manager, designer, and a bunch of other people."

**The free concept close:**
> "Before you say no — I already made a website concept for your shop. It's 100% free to look at, basically just the design and layout I came up with based on your business. It costs you nothing to check it out. I can send you the link just so you can see it.
>
> If you like it and think it could help your shop, we can talk about moving forward. If not, no problem at all — I just wanted to show you what I had in mind."

**The feedback fallback:**
> "Even if it's not something you want to move forward with, I'd still genuinely appreciate your opinion on the design. I don't take feedback personally. If there's something you don't like, or something that would make it more useful for an auto shop, that feedback could help me make a better version for a future client."

**The ask:**
> "What's the best number to send the website preview to?"

## Why This Script Works
It never opens as a sales call. It opens as a customer telling a business how they lost money, which is information the owner actually wants. By the time the pitch arrives, the problem has already been proven with a real example.
